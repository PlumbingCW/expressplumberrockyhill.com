If your sump pump is silent and water is rising in the pit, treat it as an emergency. Our dispatch line is open 24/7, nights and weekends included, and same-day appointments are available when schedules allow. Keep everyone out of the wet basement, stay away from outlets and cords near the water, and call us while you take the steps below.

Most pump calls come down to a few causes: a float switch that is stuck, a pump that has burned out, a clogged intake or a discharge line that is blocked or frozen. A plumber can tell which one it is in minutes. Some pumps can be repaired on the spot, and others are worn out and need a replacement.

When a sump pump failure is an emergency

A pit that is full and a pump that does not start is the clearest emergency. The water has nowhere to go but onto the floor. Heavy rain, fast snowmelt and a pump that runs constantly without lowering the level are also reasons to call right away.

A pump that cycles on and off every few seconds, or one that makes a grinding or rattling sound, is warning you. Those pumps often fail within days. Calling early lets us replace a failing pump on a dry floor instead of a wet one.

While you wait for the plumber

  1. Do not walk into standing water if the basement has outlets, a panel or a plugged-in appliance. If water touches electrical equipment, stay out and call 911.
  2. Check that the pump is plugged in and the outlet or breaker has not tripped, but only from dry ground.
  3. Lift stored boxes, rugs and cardboard off the floor and away from the wet area.
  4. If the pit is overflowing, a wet-dry vacuum or bucket can slow the rise. Keep the vacuum cord out of the water.
  5. Tell us the pump type, whether it hums or is silent, and how high the water is in the pit.

Float switches and why they fail

The float switch tells the pump when to start and stop. It rises with the water, and at a set height it closes the circuit. If it sticks against the pit wall, tangles in the power cord or fills with silt, the pump never gets the signal.

A float is a cheap part and often the whole problem. If the pump itself is old, though, a new float on a worn motor only delays the next failure. We check the motor and the pump housing before deciding whether the float is enough.

Battery backup for power outages

Storms that fill the pit also knock out power. A pump on household current stops at the moment you need it most. A battery backup system adds a second pump, a battery and a charger so the pit keeps draining during an outage.

A backup pump only runs as long as the battery lasts, so the size of the battery and the amount of inflow decide how many hours you get. Batteries wear out too, so they should be tested and replaced on a schedule. We can add a backup to an existing pit or install a primary and backup pair together.

New sump pump installation

A planned install costs less in stress than an emergency swap. These are the pieces we look at.

The pit

The pit needs to be deep and wide enough that the pump does not short cycle. A cracked or undersized liner is replaced or reset.

The pump type

A submersible pump sits in the pit and runs quieter. A pedestal pump has its motor above the pit and is easier to reach but louder.

The check valve

A check valve on the discharge keeps pumped water from flowing back into the pit after each cycle.

The electrical supply

The pump needs a dedicated, properly protected outlet. Any electrical work beyond plugging in is done by a licensed electrician.

Discharge lines: where the water goes

The discharge pipe carries water out of the house and away from the foundation. If it ends too close to the wall, the water soaks back into the soil and returns to the pit. If it ends in a spot that freezes, ice can block the outlet and force the pump to strain against it.

Discharge should not be tied into the sanitary sewer. Where the water goes, and what the town allows, is a question for the Rocky Hill building department. We route the line to carry water well away from the house and keep it clear of ice where we can.

Waterproofing basics for a damp basement

A sump pump handles water that has already reached the foundation. Cutting down how much arrives makes the pump's job easier. We do not run a separate waterproofing page, so here are the basics.

Start outside. Grading should slope away from the house, and gutters and downspouts should move roof water several feet from the wall. Many wet basements are a gutter problem. Clean gutters, extend downspouts and add soil where the ground has settled against the foundation.

Interior drainage

Interior drain systems collect water at the footing and send it to the sump pit. They suit basements that seep along the floor edge. A pit and pump are the exit for that system, so the pump has to be sized for the extra flow.

What we do and do not do

We repair and install pumps, pits and discharge piping. Exterior excavation, wall coatings and masonry crack repair belong to waterproofing and foundation contractors, and we will tell you when that is the better fix.

Keeping a working pump working

Pour a bucket of water into the pit twice a year and watch the pump start, drain it and shut off. Listen for odd noises. Pull out leaves, gravel and debris you can see, with the pump unplugged and your hands dry.

If the pit is wet only after storms, the pump may sit idle for months. Idle pumps seize. Testing keeps the motor free and shows a stuck float before the next storm. If the water in the pit smells like sewage, call us. That points to a different problem, and our sewer line repair page explains it.

Questions homeowners ask

Do you handle sump pump emergencies at night or on weekends in Rocky Hill?

Yes. The dispatch line answers 24/7, including nights, weekends and holidays, and storms often hit then. Tell us the pump type and the water level and we book the first plumber who can reach you. After-hours visits are priced differently, and we give the price before work starts.

How long does a sump pump last?

Many pumps last roughly 7 to 10 years, but a pump that cycles often wears out sooner, and one that sits idle can seize. It varies by model and use. If yours is that age or older, replacing it before a storm is cheaper than doing it in standing water.

My pump runs but the water is not going down. What is wrong?

The discharge line may be blocked, frozen or kinked, the check valve may be stuck, or the pump may be too small for the inflow. A pump that is running constantly can also burn out. Turn it off if it is overheating and call us.

Do I need a battery backup?

If your pit fills during storms and the power goes out with them, a backup is worth considering. It keeps one pump running for a limited time on battery. We can size it to your pit and inflow and explain how long it will run.

Can a sump pump fix a wet basement on its own?

Not always. A pump removes water from the pit, but wet walls, window wells and poor grading are separate issues. Gutters, downspouts and the slope of the soil matter too. We tell you what the pump can do and when another contractor is the right call.
